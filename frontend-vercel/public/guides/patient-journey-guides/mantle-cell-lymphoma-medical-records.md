## Content

A family can send hundreds of scanned pages and still leave a lymphoma specialist unable to answer a basic question: why was the last treatment stopped? The difficulty is often the connection between documents. A pathology report establishes what was sampled. A scan records disease at a particular time. A discharge summary describes one admission. Someone needs to make their sequence visible without changing their meaning.

For a mantle cell lymphoma consultation in China, a useful record package shows the evidence behind the diagnosis, the treatment actually delivered, the reason each approach ended, and the decision now being requested. It preserves uncertainty where the original team was uncertain. Families can organize that information without attempting to make a new diagnosis or decide eligibility themselves.

## Give the specialist a short entry point

Start with a dated introduction containing the patient's identifying details, diagnosis date, treating hospitals, and a contact for the current clinical team. State the purpose of the consultation in ordinary language. Reviewing an initial treatment recommendation, finding an alternative after a poorly tolerated medicine, and assessing a possible cellular therapy referral are different requests. A general wish for the best treatment does not help staff direct the file.

Add the treatment being taken now and the date of the most recent infusion or other procedure. Describe recent developments that could affect scheduling. A current fever, transfusion requirement, significant bleeding, or breathing difficulty belongs in direct communication with the treating team, with prompt local assessment when appropriate. Such information should not wait unnoticed in a large attachment.

End the introduction with a few decisions you need help making and the document relevant to each one. If the date or reason for an event is unknown, write that it needs confirmation. An honest gap is more useful than a guessed answer. Update this page after a new treatment or admission so that different hospitals do not receive contradictory versions described as current.

## Keep pathology tied to the specimen

Include the first diagnostic report, any supplementary reports, and later expert reviews. Each should retain the specimen date, body site, laboratory, and pathology reference number. A later biopsy from another site needs its own entry. Combining several reports into one shortened account can hide an important change or make findings from different tissues appear to come from the same specimen.

Mantle cell lymphoma diagnosis draws on morphology, immunophenotype, and relevant genetic findings. Preserve the actual descriptions of Cyclin D1, SOX11, Ki-67, and any blastoid or pleomorphic features rather than replacing them with a family interpretation. A test that was not performed must remain unperformed, not become negative in the summary. The EHA–EU MCL guideline supports expert pathology review and appropriate tissue assessment.[1]

A report is also different from the tissue material that may be needed for review. Ask the receiving Chinese hospital whether it requires stained slides, unstained sections, a block, or an accepted digital format, and how those materials are registered. Then arrange release with the original pathology department. Keep a record of what was borrowed, where it was sent, and any return arrangements. NCI explains the role of preserved tissue and pathology reports; a patient's experience of sending a specimen does not establish the transport requirements for your own route.[2]

## Send the complete molecular report

Do not reduce a molecular document to a statement such as TP53 positive. If sequencing was performed, preserve the specimen source, date, method, findings, interpretation, and stated limitations. If the only available result is p53 immunohistochemistry, label it as a staining result. Translating it into TP53 mutation changes the evidence. These assessments are not interchangeable.[1]

The same care applies to broader panels. A variant of uncertain significance is not an established reason to use a particular medicine. A negative report also has a defined testing range and method; it does not exclude every possible change. Tumor biomarker testing and testing for inherited cancer susceptibility serve different purposes. A change found in tumor tissue does not by itself establish an inherited condition in the family.[3]

When results from separate dates differ, include both reports and identify their context, such as the original lymph node sample and a biopsy obtained at relapse. The consulting team can then consider specimen differences, testing methods, and changes in the disease. Families do not need to choose a preferred result before asking for advice.

## Make imaging comparisons possible

For major imaging assessments, retain both the written report and the original images in a format the receiving hospital can use, commonly DICOM. Photographs of selected printed images may omit the information a radiologist needs. Check that downloaded files or discs open before the consultation, but avoid altering the original medical images to make a smaller attachment.

Identify the assessment before treatment, relevant assessments during treatment, the end-of-treatment assessment, and any investigation of suspected relapse. Include the scanning institution, date, and examination type. A label such as latest scan is easily lost when another examination is performed. A date and description remain useful when several hospitals are comparing the record.[4]

Connect a recorded response to its supporting assessment. If a summary says complete remission, identify when that conclusion was reached and where the underlying reports can be found. A statement that a PET scan was negative does not show every site that was involved initially. Relevant marrow and gastrointestinal reports should remain available when they contributed to the original staging or later assessment; do not remove them merely because a recent scan was reassuring.[1,4]

## Explain the treatment sequence, including interruptions

Create a chronological treatment table with the regimen, start and end dates, cycles actually received or duration of oral therapy, best documented response, and reason for ending or changing the treatment. Several cycles of one regimen are not automatically several treatment lines. Record the factual sequence and let the clinicians confirm how it should be classified.

The reason for stopping often matters as much as the drug name. Disease progression while taking a medicine, a pause during infection, unacceptable bleeding, financial interruption, and completion of the intended course are distinct events. For a BTK inhibitor in particular, intolerance and documented progression should not both be translated as resistance. Attach the clinical note or assessment explaining the change whenever it is available.

Accurate prior exposure may affect discussion of a later treatment. The Chinese pirtobrutinib prescribing information, for example, describes an indication involving previous systemic treatment and a BTK inhibitor.[5] That makes the actual prior medication history relevant. It does not give the patient a reason to relabel treatment lines, and a well-organized table cannot substitute for a clinician's eligibility assessment.

## Reconcile the medicines being taken today

List the generic name, formulation, strength, and current prescribed instructions for each anticancer drug. Include the prescription or dispensing record where possible. If an overseas brand name might be unfamiliar, a clear photograph of the original packaging can help identify it, while the prescriber or pharmacist confirms the medicine itself.

Add other prescriptions, over-the-counter products, herbal remedies, and supplements. Anticoagulants, antiplatelet medicines, and recent anti-infective treatments should not disappear from the record because the consultation concerns lymphoma. A medication review needs to know what is actually being used, including products that the patient does not regard as medicine.

Current acalabrutinib labeling illustrates why this matters: interactions can change drug exposure, and bleeding and other adverse effects require clinical attention.[6] The action depends on the particular product, accompanying medicines, and individual circumstances. Compiling the list should lead to a pharmacist or clinician review, not an unsupervised decision to stop a cardiovascular medicine or change the anticancer prescription.

## Preserve laboratory context and recent complications

Keep test dates, units, reference ranges, and the original laboratory reports. A separate trend sheet can make repeated blood counts easier to read, but it should point back to the originals. If one laboratory uses a different unit or reporting method, do not silently convert values in a way that might introduce errors.

Include recent transfusions, hospital-treated infections, kidney or liver changes, and a substantial decline in daily function on the same timeline as treatment. An isolated result obtained after recovery may not reveal the burden of the preceding month. Notes about what the patient could do before and after an admission can also help the consulting team understand the clinical account.

Retain the specific names and results of hepatitis B tests rather than summarizing them as hepatitis normal. HBsAg, anti-HBs, and total anti-HBc provide different information, as explained by CDC.[7] If an earlier team prescribed antiviral treatment or monitoring, include that plan and the responsible clinician. The new team needs the evidence and existing instructions before deciding whether anything should change.

## Build a separate transplant or CAR-T section when relevant

A transplant record should identify whether the procedure was autologous or allogeneic, the conditioning treatment, infusion date, recovery course, important complications, and current preventive care. A generic entry saying bone marrow transplant can obscure clinically important differences. Include unresolved problems and the monitoring still being arranged. NCI describes transplantation as a process involving treatment and recovery, rather than a single admission whose discharge ends all care.[8]

For CAR-T therapy, record the actual product or research protocol, collection and infusion dates, bridging therapy, lymphodepleting treatment, and significant adverse events. Preserve the clinical documentation of cytokine release syndrome, neurologic problems, or other complications instead of relying on a remembered severity label. Ask the original center to state the surveillance and precautions that remain relevant.

FDA's Tecartus material describes serious risks that help explain the importance of that history.[9] It is United States product information, not evidence that a particular Chinese hospital offers the same product. The receiving team must assess the patient's actual prior treatment and local options. If treatment occurred in a study, retain the study name, registration number, investigator contact, and outstanding follow-up obligations as well.

## Translate meaning without resolving uncertainty yourself

Keep the original report beside its translation or link both through a simple document number. Use an unambiguous date format and generic drug names. Preserve units and the distinction between a tablet strength and the total prescribed dose. The aim is for a clinician to trace an important statement back to its source quickly.

Words such as suspected, possible, and consistent with should retain their degree of uncertainty. A pending examination remains pending. A family summary should not transform either into a confirmed diagnosis or a negative finding. When a term is difficult to translate, retaining the original wording with a note is preferable to choosing a more definite medical claim.

Confirm language and file requirements through the receiving hospital's official channel. NCI's information on finding cancer care describes the role of additional opinions, but administrative acceptance of a file is not the same as completing a diagnostic consultation or agreeing to provide treatment.[10] Keep one untouched set of originals, one navigable index, and a dated summary. Share them with the people involved in care and check that essential attachments can actually be opened.

Before leaving China, apply the same method to the new records: add the revised assessment, treatment delivered, current prescriptions, pending results, and the next review arrangements. A useful handover allows the next clinician to continue the work with a clear account of what happened, what remains uncertain, and who will address it.
