## Key Takeaways

- Sort everything into four groups: never-check, first-hospital-day, local-recovery and replaceable. Sorting by clothes versus toiletries misses the point.
- Essential medicines, a delay reserve, prescriptions, allergy information and critical supplies travel with you in original labelled packaging, within the limits of law and airline rules.
- Ask the hospital what it supplies, what it bans and what it expects you to bring. Rules on medicines, electrical equipment, food, bedding and companion items change from one campus or ward to the next.
- Oxygen, CPAP, pumps, powered mobility devices and batteries need advance clearance from every carrier on the route.
- Pathology, biological material and large medicine quantities fall outside ordinary luggage rules. Arrange compliant transfer well before you reach the airport.

## Content

A cheap item in a suitcase can turn out to be the most important one if you cannot replace it on arrival. A heavy “just in case” bag works against you: more falls, more fatigue, harder transfers.

So pack by **failure consequence**. Picture the item missing for 24–72 hours — what happens then? That answer tells you whether it stays on the patient, goes in carry-on, can be checked, or has no business travelling at all.

### Layer 1: never check these items

Everything critical goes in one small, water-resistant carry-on that the patient or an authorised companion can physically handle:

- passport, visa/entry material, tickets and emergency contacts;
- hospital, department, campus, appointment/admission and payment contacts;
- one-page medical summary, medicines/allergies, important device information and insurance;
- essential prescription medicines plus a clinician-approved delay reserve;
- rescue medicines such as an inhaler or epinephrine auto-injector when prescribed;
- time-critical consumables such as ostomy pouches, glucose supplies or catheter items;
- phone, charging cable, safe plug adapter and essential device batteries;
- one change of accessible clothing and basic hygiene items.

For travellers with chronic illness, the CDC tells you to carry the medicines and supplies you need in their original containers along with your medical information, and to keep the health kit in your carry-on baggage [1]. Its advice for falling ill abroad points the same way: original labelled containers and enough medicine to cover delays, with nothing essential in checked baggage [2].

One traveller should never hold every critical item. Split secure copies and a small backup supply between the patient and the authorised companion — but work out who carries which doses, or the split can set up a duplicate-dosing mistake.

### Pack medicines as a verified system

Each medicine gets a full record: generic and brand names, strength, formulation, route, dose, timing, indication, storage and prescriber. Keep the original pharmacy label on it, and carry a clinician letter for injections, controlled drugs, unusual quantities or devices. The CDC's travel-health-kit guidance asks for a diagnosis/medicine/allergy summary and suggests tailoring supplies to your history, itinerary, trip duration and access to care [3].

Confirm all of this before you travel:

1. whether the medicine may be brought into China and through transit countries;
2. the amount permitted and documents required;
3. whether refrigeration or light protection is needed;
4. the dosing plan across time zones and fasting/procedure instructions;
5. whether the Chinese hospital will continue, substitute, hold or re-prescribe it after reconciliation.

Rules differ from country to country, and some narcotic or psychoactive products are restricted. The CDC recommends checking destination law and documenting the generic name, dose and indication [4]. A prescription from home, on its own, proves nothing about import permission.

Different tablets in one unlabelled organiser will cause trouble at the border. Keep everything in labelled packaging until each medicine's identity is preserved and the hospital has reconciled the list — the organiser comes after that. And don't share your medicines with a companion.

### Protect cold-chain medicines without guessing

Get the permitted temperature range and excursion time from the manufacturer or pharmacist; “keep cool” is not a specification. Use an approved insulated system that guards against freezing as much as overheating, and pack a thermometer or logger only when it is clinically appropriate and airline-compliant. Refrigeration at every hotel and hospital stop needs to be arranged before you leave.

Insulin, biologics and other irreplaceable temperature-sensitive medicines should never sit directly against loose ice. If an excursion happens, record it and ask a pharmacist or the manufacturer before using the medicine — looking normal proves nothing about potency.

### Pre-clear devices and batteries

Build a device card for each piece of equipment: manufacturer, model, serial number, battery type/Watt-hour rating, mains input, clinical purpose, what the alarms mean, consumables and a technical contact. Then ask every operating carrier about advance notice, medical clearance, cabin use and security screening — the company that sold you the ticket may not be the one flying the plane.

IATA's dangerous-goods guidance puts portable medical electronic devices and spare lithium batteries under specific carry-on, capacity, approval and protection rules [5]. Chinese civil-aviation standards also require prior arrangements and carrier approval for certain powered wheelchairs, and they restrict spare medical-device batteries [6]. The rules shift with the device and the battery, so another airline's website can never tell you whether this flight will accept yours.

Portable oxygen concentrators, CPAP/BiPAP machines, infusion pumps, suction units, ventilatory support and powered mobility equipment each need a failure plan: enough approved battery time, protected terminals, spare consumables, a manual alternative where one exists, and power compatibility at the destination. Aircraft emergency oxygen covers the aircraft's emergency; it was never meant to stand in for your pre-approved personal requirement.

### Layer 2: the first hospital day bag

Pack only the things you will need before a room or your checked bag is available:

- original identity and hospital confirmation;
- current source records or secure access instructions;
- medication list and the day's doses, clearly separated;
- glasses, hearing aids/dentures and labelled cases;
- mobility aid and safe non-slip footwear;
- simple, loose clothing that permits examination or access to the treated area;
- communication aid, translation glossary, pen and small notebook;
- charger and modest cash/payment backup.

Leave jewellery, large amounts of cash, irreplaceable sentimental items and valuables you cannot keep under control at home. Photograph the luggage contents if you like, but keep sensitive record pages out of the frame.

### Ask the ward before packing the inpatient bag

Supply and safety policies differ from hospital to hospital — sometimes from ward to ward. Ask the exact ward you will be staying in:

- Are gown, linens, towels, toiletries, slippers and patient meals provided?
- May home medicines stay at bedside, or must they be handed to staff?
- Are kettles, heating pads, extension leads, humidifiers or other appliances prohibited?
- Can CPAP, glucose monitors or pumps connect to hospital systems or require inspection?
- Are outside food, flowers, latex, aerosols or strong fragrances restricted?
- What can an overnight companion bring, and is bedside storage available?

Then pack to the answers you get. Another hospital allowing extra blankets or an appliance says nothing about this one. Label removable aids, but keep the full diagnosis off anything visible on the outside of your luggage.

### Layer 3: local recovery

Choose each item by the function you expect to have:

- front-opening or loose clothing for restricted shoulders, ports, drains or casts;
- elastic waistbands and easy footwear for swelling or reduced bending;
- clinician-approved compression or braces—not self-prescribed substitutes;
- familiar adaptive cutlery, reacher or toileting aid only if safe and cleanable;
- measured nutrition items when the clinical team approves them;
- a small laundry system and separate bag for soiled dressings or clothing.

Leave the unreviewed pharmacy at home: supplements, herbal products, sleeping aids, pain medicines. Any of them can interact with anaesthesia, anticoagulants, cancer treatment or laboratory interpretation. Every product you intend to bring goes on the pre-admission list.

### Do not carry specimens as casual baggage

Glass pathology slides, paraffin blocks, frozen material, blood, cells and other biological substances can trigger a long list of requirements: institutional release, packaging, permits, temperature control, chain of custody, compliant courier handling. China's customs rules regulate entry/exit health quarantine for special biological articles, and certain self-use materials require medical proof [7]. None of that entitles a patient to drop a specimen into a suitcase.

The safe route is hospital-to-hospital or laboratory-to-laboratory transfer. You carry the reports and tracking documents; the physical material travels only when the sending institution, receiving institution, carrier and authorities have all confirmed the route in writing.

### Create a replacement map

For each critical item, decide in advance how you would replace it:

| Item | If lost/delayed | Proof needed |
|---|---|---|
| medicine | prescriber + verified pharmacy/hospital | prescription and generic name |
| ostomy/device supply | manufacturer/distributor + hospital | model and size |
| glasses/hearing aid | local provider + spare | prescription/model |
| records | encrypted backup + source institution | identity and authorisation |
| passport | consulate + local police/immigration process | secure copies and report |

If you need a replacement medicine, buy only from a seller you can verify. The CDC warns that storage, labelling and authenticity can be unreliable and recommends knowing how to obtain a safe replacement [2].

### Run a door-to-bed rehearsal

Two days before departure, pack everything and rehearse the whole journey: leaving home, security screening, six hours without checked luggage, hospital registration, one missed connection, the first night after discharge. If the patient cannot lift or roll the bag safely, arrange assistance or take things out.

Check expiry dates, quantities, device function, battery ratings, file access, the hospital address and the weather. Photograph the closed bags and the final packing manifest. After that, nothing goes in that has not passed the same medicine or device review as everything else.

**Medical, customs and aviation disclaimer:** Packing rules vary by medicine, device, airline, route, customs authority, hospital and clinical plan. This guide does not authorise import, cabin use or bedside use. Obtain written confirmation from the responsible parties.

## FAQ

### Should essential medicines go in checked luggage?

No — essential medicines and necessary supplies stay in your carry-on, in original labelled containers, with a clinically appropriate delay reserve and supporting documents, within legal and airline limits.

### Can I bring my home medicines into a Chinese ward?

Bring them so the team can reconcile them, and ask the ward how they will be stored and administered. During the admission you do not dose yourself from your luggage unless the clinical team has explicitly authorised a process for it.

### May I use my CPAP or oxygen concentrator on the flight and in hospital?

Only after the device, batteries, airline approval, power and hospital inspection requirements have all been checked. Cabin approval and bedside integration are separate questions — neither one automatically grants the other.

### Can I hand-carry pathology slides or samples?

Only through a compliant process confirmed in advance. Physical specimens can require release, packaging, permits, chain of custody and a qualified courier — they are never ordinary personal baggage by default.
