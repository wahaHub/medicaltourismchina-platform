## Key Takeaways

- A responsible TCM consultation includes the main complaint, medical diagnoses, tests, operations, allergies, pregnancy status and every medicine or supplement—not only tongue and pulse findings.
- Traditional “pattern differentiation” and a biomedical diagnosis answer different questions; one should not silently replace the other.
- Acupuncture, herbal medicines, moxibustion, cupping and tuina are separate interventions with separate evidence, risks and consent.
- A herbal prescription should identify every ingredient, amount, processing requirement, preparation method, dosing schedule and planned duration.
- International patients should obtain a bilingual record and check destination-country rules before travelling with herbs, animal products or medicines.

## Content

A traditional Chinese medicine consultation may feel familiar in some ways and unfamiliar in others. There should still be registration, identification, a clinical history, examination, a working assessment, discussion of options and a written plan. The distinctive elements may include questions about temperature preference, appetite, sleep and bowel patterns, inspection of the tongue, palpation of the pulse and a TCM “pattern” formulation. These observations should be understood in their own clinical framework; they do not replace emergency triage, pathology, imaging or medication review when those are needed.

### Before the appointment

Bring a one-page medical summary, the current diagnosis, recent relevant reports, allergies and a complete list of prescription drugs, over-the-counter medicines, herbal products, vitamins and supplements. Include the actual dose and frequency—not just a photograph of mixed tablets. Note pregnancy or breastfeeding, kidney or liver disease, bleeding risk, immune suppression, planned surgery and previous reactions to herbs or needling.

Write down the main goal in ordinary language: fewer migraine days, less nausea, easier bowel movements, better sleep or improved hand function. Also list any warning symptoms or recent changes. Severe chest pain, sudden neurological deficit, major bleeding, high fever, anaphylaxis, acute breathing difficulty or another emergency needs local emergency care before a routine TCM appointment.

Confirm the institution’s legal Chinese name and the physician’s full Chinese name. China’s TCM Law requires people providing TCM medical care to hold the relevant physician qualification and registration, and clinics must operate within their licensed or filed scope [1]. Physician registration records practice category and scope [2]. A hotel wellness service or retail herb shop should not be assumed to be a medical consultation.

### What the physician may ask

The interview should start with the symptom timeline and what has already been diagnosed or treated. Expect questions about pain, energy, sleep, appetite, thirst, digestion, urination, bowel movements, menstrual and reproductive history where relevant, temperature sensitivity, sweating, mood and how the condition affects daily life. Some questions help traditional pattern differentiation; others identify contraindications or a need for conventional investigation.

Do not omit prescription medicines because the visit is “natural.” The physician needs to know about anticoagulants, antiplatelet drugs, diabetes medicines, immunosuppressants, sedatives, antiseizure drugs, chemotherapy, targeted therapy and medicines with a narrow therapeutic range. Bring the names of previous herbal formulas and why they were stopped.

A good consultation makes room for uncertainty. If the practitioner cannot explain a new mass, unexplained weight loss, persistent fever, jaundice, neurological loss or severe progressive pain, the next step may be referral or testing rather than immediate treatment.

### Tongue, pulse and physical examination

The practitioner may observe the tongue body and coating and palpate the radial pulse at both wrists, along with the face, voice, breathing, skin, abdomen or painful area. These findings contribute to TCM pattern differentiation. They should be documented in understandable terms, but they are not laboratory measurements and should not be presented as proof of organ failure, infection, cancer or hormone levels.

The visit may also include conventional observations such as blood pressure, temperature, neurological screening, range of movement or abdominal examination when relevant. Ask which findings are TCM descriptors and which are biomedical diagnoses. The distinction prevents a translated term such as “liver,” “kidney” or “spleen” in a TCM pattern from being mistaken for a confirmed disease of that organ.

### The assessment: diagnosis and pattern are not synonyms

At the end of assessment, the physician may record both a biomedical diagnosis and one or more TCM patterns. The pattern is a traditional synthesis used to select treatment and may change as symptoms change. It is not necessarily a permanent label, and two practitioners may formulate it differently.

Ask the physician to explain:

- what condition or symptom is being treated;
- the TCM pattern in plain language;
- whether additional tests or another specialty are needed;
- the expected benefit and the time by which it should be noticeable;
- what the treatment will not do;
- reasonable alternatives, including no TCM treatment.

Mixed or uncertain evidence should be described honestly. The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health notes that studies of Chinese herbal products have produced mixed results and that safety problems have included undeclared ingredients, heavy metals, pesticides, microorganisms and incorrect herbs [3]. “Used for centuries” does not establish the quality of the product in hand or its effectiveness for this patient.

### Treatment options require separate decisions

A consultation may lead to acupuncture, an herbal decoction, granules, a manufactured Chinese patent medicine, moxibustion, cupping, tuina, tai chi or dietary advice. Consent to one is not consent to the others.

For each proposed intervention, ask for the target symptom, relevant evidence, common burdens, material risks, alternatives, cost, number of sessions or doses and stopping rule. Acupuncture needs sterile single-use needles and technique-specific precautions. Moxibustion adds heat, burn and smoke exposure. Cupping can bruise or injure skin. Tuina may be unsuitable over fracture, unstable spine, acute inflammation, thrombosis or certain postoperative sites. Herbal products require ingredient and interaction review.

Avoid packages that combine many modalities so quickly that neither benefit nor adverse effects can be attributed. Starting one major change at a time, when clinically reasonable, makes follow-up more informative.

### What a usable herbal prescription looks like

China’s prescription rules require standardised prescription information and specify that Chinese decoction-piece prescriptions should record ingredients and legal units, with special processing or decoction instructions such as “decoct first,” “add later” or “wrap” [4]. Hospital management rules also require review of pregnancy cautions, recognised incompatibilities and doses that exceed usual ranges [5].

Before leaving, obtain a copy showing:

- patient name, date, diagnosis and prescriber identity;
- every ingredient’s exact Chinese name and amount per dose;
- species or source details when confusion is possible;
- processing form and any special order of decoction;
- number of daily doses, total days and timing with food or medicines;
- how much water to use, how long to soak and boil, how to divide and store the liquid;
- the dispensing pharmacy or hospital and batch information for manufactured products;
- what to do after a missed dose, vomiting or an adverse reaction.

Do not accept an unlabelled bag or a translation that says only “detox herbs.” If the formula changes at follow-up, keep the old and new versions with dates. For granules or patent medicines, photograph the sealed package, manufacturer, approval or registration information, ingredient list, strength, lot and expiry.

### Check interactions and medical vulnerabilities

“Natural” substances are pharmacologically active. Ask the TCM physician and, where possible, a pharmacist or the prescribing specialist to review the full combination. Liver or kidney disease, pregnancy, childhood, older age, frailty and multiple medicines increase the importance of dose and monitoring. People receiving cancer therapy, transplantation medicines or anticoagulation should not add a formula without the treating team knowing.

Do not stop a prescribed medicine because a practitioner calls it “toxic” or says the herb will replace it. If a change is proposed, the original prescriber should participate and a monitoring plan should be written.

Stop the new product and seek timely medical advice for jaundice, dark urine, severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, facial or throat swelling, breathing difficulty, blistering rash, fainting, unusual bleeding, marked confusion or a rapid deterioration. Bring the actual product and prescription to the assessment. Suspected adverse reactions should be documented and reported through the treating institution’s pharmacovigilance process.

### Follow-up should test the original goal

The review visit should compare the same baseline measure: symptom days, pain interference, bowel frequency, sleep awakenings, rescue-medication use or a defined daily task. The physician should also ask about new symptoms and check whether other treatment changed.

If the target has not improved by the agreed point, reconsider the diagnosis, adherence, treatment or decision to continue. A changing pulse or tongue description alone is not enough to justify indefinite prescribing when the patient’s meaningful outcome has not changed. Long courses should have a reason, safety monitoring and periodic attempts to simplify where appropriate.

### International documentation and travelling with products

Request an English or bilingual note that lists the diagnosis, TCM pattern, complete prescription, treatment dates, response, adverse effects and follow-up plan. Machine-translated ingredient names are not reliable enough for allergy, interaction or customs decisions; retain the Chinese original and use botanical or pharmaceutical names where available.

Check the destination country, airline and transit-country rules before purchase. China Customs states that outbound herbs and Chinese patent medicines are generally managed as reasonable quantities for personal use when they are not prohibited or restricted, while some materials—including products involving protected or restricted animal and plant substances—may not be taken out [6]. The destination may impose additional medicine, plant-health or wildlife restrictions. Keep products in original labelled packaging with the prescription; do not ship loose mixed herbs without checking the rules.

**Medical disclaimer:** This guide provides general educational information, not a diagnosis, prescription or endorsement of TCM. Discuss herbs and procedures with clinicians who know the full medical history. Seek urgent local care for severe or rapidly worsening symptoms.

## FAQ

### Will every TCM consultation include tongue and pulse examination?

They are common, but the exact examination depends on the practitioner and problem. They should sit alongside an adequate medical history and appropriate conventional assessment, not replace urgent tests or specialist review.

### What is the difference between a TCM pattern and a biomedical diagnosis?

A TCM pattern is a traditional synthesis used to guide treatment; a biomedical diagnosis identifies disease using clinical criteria and, when needed, tests or imaging. The terms may coexist but are not interchangeable.

### Should I tell the TCM doctor about all supplements and prescription medicines?

Yes. Bring exact names, doses and schedules. Herbal ingredients can interact with medicines or add liver, kidney, bleeding, sedation, glucose or immune effects.

### Can I take an unlabelled herbal mixture home?

It is not a safe or practical choice. Obtain the complete prescription and labelled packaging, then check export, destination and transit rules; loose products may be impossible to identify clinically or at customs.

### How soon should a herbal formula be reviewed?

The prescriber should set the interval according to the condition, formula and risk. New formulas usually need a defined early review of benefit, tolerance and interactions rather than automatic long-term refills.
