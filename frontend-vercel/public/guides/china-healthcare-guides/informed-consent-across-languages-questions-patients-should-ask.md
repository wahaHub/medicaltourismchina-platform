## Key Takeaways

- Chinese law requires clinicians to explain the condition and medical measures; before surgery, special examinations or special treatment, material risks and alternatives must be explained and explicit consent obtained.[1]
- A signed Chinese form does not cure a failed conversation. Hospitals are expected to communicate in a way and language the patient can understand.[2][3]
- Use a trained medical interpreter for material decisions when the patient and clinician do not share enough language. A coordinator or relative should not silently edit the message.
- Ask what is proposed, why now, what benefit is realistic, what important harms may occur, what alternatives exist and what happens if the patient waits or declines.
- If the plan changes, consent may need to be revisited. Emergency exceptions are narrow and should not be treated as routine convenience.[1]

## Content

A consent form can be signed in thirty seconds. Informed consent cannot.

For an international patient, the weak point is often not the absence of information but the way it moves: the surgeon speaks Chinese, a coordinator produces a short English summary, a relative says “it is standard,” and the patient signs a form they cannot read. Each person may be trying to help, yet the patient still has not had a direct, reliable explanation.

China’s Civil Code requires medical staff to explain the condition and measures. For surgery, special examination or special treatment, they must timely explain medical risks and alternative plans and obtain explicit consent.[1] National Health Commission rules also call for risk communication in a manner and language the patient can understand.[2] The practical question is therefore not merely “Was a form signed?” but “Could this patient understand and make a voluntary choice?”

## The Four Parts of a Real Consent Process

### Information

The patient receives enough relevant information about the proposed intervention, expected benefit, important risks, alternatives and uncertainty.

### Understanding

The explanation uses language and a format the patient can follow. Numbers, anatomy and technical words are clarified; misunderstanding is checked rather than assumed away.

### Voluntary choice

The patient can ask questions, take reasonable time when the situation permits and decide without deceptive guarantees or improper pressure.

### Documentation

The discussion and decision are recorded, with the correct patient, procedure, site, date and participants. A signature documents a process; it does not replace one. The National Health Commission has similarly described written consent as both confirmation and a process of explaining the plan, risks and alternatives.[3]

## Arrange Language Support Before the Consent Desk

Tell the hospital the patient’s preferred spoken and written language. They may differ. A patient may speak English comfortably but read medical information more accurately in Arabic, Russian or another language.

Ask who will interpret. A trained medical interpreter should convey the complete conversation in both directions, preserve uncertainty and ask for clarification when a word or number is unclear. A coordinator may arrange the meeting but should not decide which clinical sentences “matter.”

Family members can provide history and emotional support. They can also soften bad news, omit reproductive or mental-health information, or answer on the patient’s behalf. For a material decision, place a qualified interpreter between the languages and let the family remain family.

If no in-person interpreter is available, ask about video or telephone interpretation. For urgent care, the team should use the safest available communication method and improve language support as quickly as possible; essential treatment should not be dangerously delayed for an ideal arrangement.

## Eleven Questions Worth Asking

The following list is short enough to use in a consultation and broad enough to expose gaps.

1. **What exactly are you proposing?** Ask for the procedure, body part, side, technique and intended result.
2. **What problem is it meant to solve?** A treatment can control symptoms, prevent deterioration, establish a diagnosis or aim for cure; these are not interchangeable goals.
3. **How likely is the intended benefit for someone like this patient?** Ask for a range and time frame, not only “high success.”
4. **What are the important risks?** Include common burdens and uncommon but serious harm, as well as anaesthesia, transfusion or contrast risk where relevant.
5. **What alternatives are reasonable?** Ask about another procedure, medicines, observation, treatment elsewhere and no treatment now.
6. **What may happen if we wait or decline?** The answer helps distinguish a time-sensitive decision from a sales deadline.
7. **Who performs the procedure?** Clarify the named lead clinician, supervision and the roles of trainees or other team members.
8. **What could change the plan?** New imaging, pathology, findings during surgery or the patient’s condition may alter the approach.
9. **What recovery and follow-up are expected?** Cover pain, mobility, diet, devices, medicines, restrictions, warning signs and who answers questions after discharge.
10. **Which costs are not in the estimate?** Consent to treatment and financial agreement are related but separate; both should be understandable.
11. **What decision must be made today?** Ask what time is genuinely available for questions or a second opinion.

## Turn Percentages Into Meaning

“There is a five per cent risk” is incomplete. Five per cent of what, over what period, and according to which patient group? Does it include a minor wound problem, permanent disability or death?

Ask for absolute numbers where possible: “About how many out of 100 similar patients experience this within 30 days?” Ask whether the figure comes from published evidence, the hospital’s results or the clinician’s estimate.

For benefit, distinguish technical success from the outcome the patient values. A stent may open a vessel; an operation may remove a visible lesion; neither statement alone explains survival, symptoms or quality of life.

## Use Teach-Back, Not “Do You Understand?”

At the end, the clinician can ask the patient to explain in their own words:

- What is being done and why
- The most important risks and alternatives
- What the patient should expect afterward
- Which finding would require urgent help

The interpreter conveys the exchange without improving the patient’s answer. If the explanation is wrong, the clinical team explains again. This tests the communication, not the patient’s intelligence.

Patients can also initiate teach-back: “I want to check that I understood. My understanding is…” This is especially useful for dose schedules, surgery side, treatment intent and the difference between a preliminary and final diagnosis.

## Read the Form as a Map of the Conversation

Ask for time to review the consent form and request an English or translated version when available. If only a Chinese form is used, have the key sections interpreted aloud and ask for a written English summary of the individual plan.

Check:

- Patient name and identification number
- Diagnosis and proposed procedure
- Side, site and level where relevant
- Anaesthesia and transfusion provisions
- Implants, tissue use or blood products
- Material risks and alternatives discussed
- Permission for photography, teaching or research, if present
- Names and roles of the clinician, interpreter and representative
- Date and time

Do not sign blank spaces or a form for a different procedure “to save time.” Correct errors before signing and obtain a copy of the final version.

## Consent for Treatment Is Not Consent for Everything

A clinical procedure, research participation, publication of identifiable images, optional biobanking and marketing use are different decisions. Ask staff to separate them.

China’s rules for research involving people require voluntary informed consent and information expressed in language the participant can understand.[5] A patient should know whether an intervention is established care, off-label use, an innovative technique or part of a clinical study. “New” and “advanced” are promotional words unless the team explains evidence, approval status, oversight and alternatives.

Declining optional photography or research should not be represented as declining necessary clinical care. If the documents combine choices, ask the institution to show how each permission is recorded.

## When a Relative Signs

The patient should remain at the centre whenever they can understand, communicate and decide. A relative’s presence does not automatically transfer decision-making authority.

When a patient cannot provide consent or when disclosure to the patient is considered inappropriate under the applicable rules, the hospital may involve a close relative or legal representative. Ask staff to explain the legal basis, whose consent is being obtained and how the patient’s own wishes are being considered.[1]

Do not let convenience answer this question. “The spouse speaks Chinese” is a language fact, not proof that the spouse should make the decision.

## Plan Changes and Emergencies

Before a procedure, ask what foreseeable changes are covered. For example, if findings during surgery differ from imaging, may the team extend the operation, take a biopsy, place a device or convert techniques? The boundaries should be discussed while the patient can participate.

The Civil Code permits necessary medical measures in an emergency threatening life when the patient’s or close relative’s opinion cannot be obtained, after approval by the institution’s responsible or authorised person.[1] This exception protects emergency rescue. It is not a blanket substitute for poor scheduling, absent translation or an incomplete elective discussion.

If the treatment plan materially changes before it begins, ask for a new explanation and updated documentation. A consent form from last week does not automatically answer a different decision today.

## A Five-Minute Pause Before Signing

Use the pause to ask the patient—not the companion—to state:

- “This treatment is intended to…”
- “The main risks I am accepting are…”
- “My other reasonable options are…”
- “The plan may change if…”
- “After discharge, I should call… and seek urgent help if…”

Then confirm that the English explanation, interpreter’s words and Chinese form refer to the same procedure. If they do not, stop and reconcile the discrepancy.

**Medical and legal disclaimer:** This guide provides general educational information and is not legal advice or a substitute for consent discussions with the treating team. Capacity, representation, emergency authority and documentation requirements depend on the circumstances and applicable law. Patients should ask the hospital to explain its current procedure.

Before selecting an international service, ask which languages it supports for consent, whether trained interpreters are available after hours and how language assistance is documented.

Detailed consent is especially important for surgery, anaesthesia, invasive procedures, transfusion, fertility-related treatment, high-risk medicines, radiation and research participation.

## FAQ

### Is an English consent form legally required for every international patient in China?

The national sources cited here require understandable communication and appropriate consent, but do not establish that every hospital must stock every form in English.[1][2] Ask for trained interpretation and a written explanation the patient can review.

### Can a coordinator explain the risks instead of the doctor?

A coordinator can facilitate communication, but clinical risks, benefits and alternatives should come from the responsible clinical team. An interpreter transfers that explanation; neither role should invent clinical advice.

### May I take the form away and discuss it?

For non-emergency care, ask how much time is available and request a copy. Urgency varies, but pressure based only on travel arrangements or a sales deadline deserves scrutiny.

### What if the form and the verbal explanation do not match?

Do not sign until the treating team reconciles the procedure, side, risk or plan and corrects the document. Record the interpreter and obtain the final copy.

### Does signing mean I have accepted every possible change during surgery?

Not automatically. Ask the surgeon what foreseeable extensions or conversions are included and which decisions would require further permission. Emergency authority is circumstance-specific.[1]
