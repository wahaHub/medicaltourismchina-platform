## Key Takeaways

- Shanghai gives you three ways in: ordinary public outpatient care, the international or VIP service inside a public hospital, and the private international hospitals and clinics. Which one fits depends on the specialty, language, price, insurance and after-hours support you need — your nationality alone won't answer it.[1][3]
- Use the same passport spelling and identity number every time. A second hospital card or a duplicate digital profile can split the record of one episode of care.
- The municipal “Suishenban” app accepts foreign-passport registration for specific services, and the city's official hospital guide shows how to book appointments through its healthcare section.[2][4]
- A direct-billing logo on the wall only means the hospital can bill your insurer; it says nothing about whether this visit is approved. Confirm the facility, department, date of service, deductible and pre-authorisation with both the provider and the insurer.
- In a medical emergency in Shanghai, call 120. The city's official guide says the 120 service works in English and asks callers to give a precise address and symptoms.[5]

## Content

Shanghai looks easy at first: international clinics, big teaching hospitals and polished apps are all one search away. The hard part comes earlier — working out which layer of that system you should actually enter.

A traveller with a simple infection, an expat who needs ongoing primary care and a patient flying in for complex surgery do not belong in the same queue. So before you compare hospital names, decide which care pathway fits the problem in front of you.

## Choose One of Three Care Pathways

### Ordinary public outpatient service

This works when the department you need is clear and you can handle a mostly Chinese workflow, or you bring reliable interpretation. You get direct access to a broad specialist network. The trade-off: registration, payment, tests and the pharmacy may each sit at a different counter or in a different app.

### International or VIP service in a public hospital

This route usually adds bilingual coordination, its own booking channel, longer consultations, private rooms and help with commercial insurance. It still may not cover every department, doctor, test or emergency service the hospital has. So ask early: if you need imaging, pathology, surgery or admission, does care stay inside the international service, or does it move into the standard system?

### Private international hospital or clinic

For primary care, paediatrics, routine specialist visits — or when your insurer already has a network here — this is often the smoothest option. For a complex episode, check the hard infrastructure first: inpatient capability, intensive-care backup, pathology and imaging access, blood-bank arrangements and the transfer protocol.

Shanghai's municipal portal publishes an international medical-services directory with addresses, opening hours and contact details.[1] Use it to find doors and phone numbers; a listing is no quality ranking, and it doesn't guarantee the provider can handle your condition.

## Test the Match Before Paying for Travel

Before you book flights, send a compact case brief to the service that would actually receive you. It should state the working diagnosis, the decision you want made, current symptoms, major prior treatment, medication and allergy risks, and which source materials you can provide.

Then ask them to answer four questions in writing:

1. Which department and which service pathway would take the case?
2. Has a clinician reviewed the records, or did an administrator only check them for completeness?
3. What will have to be repeated after arrival, and why?
4. What would make the visit unsuitable, urgent or impossible to finish on the planned dates?

An invitation letter, a quotation, an appointment — none of these proves a procedure is medically indicated. Expect the preliminary opinion to shift once the team has examined you and weighed local imaging, pathology review or new laboratory results.

## Make the Digital Identity Match the Passport

Shanghai's official hospital guide describes registering with a passport, getting a hospital card on a first visit and booking appointments online through the healthcare section of “Suishenban.”[2] The city's guide to the Shanghai QR Code adds that foreign-passport users can register, with manual identity review for some functions.[4]

Test the app well before you depend on it, while a failed verification is still easy to fix. Keep these fields identical everywhere:

- surname and given names, in passport order;
- the passport number, every letter and leading zero included;
- nationality and date of birth;
- a mobile number that can receive verification codes;
- the hospital patient number, once issued.

If an old profile won't open, don't just register a fresh patient. Ask the hospital to locate the existing record or merge the profiles. Otherwise the consultation lives in one account and the imaging or laboratory results in another, and nobody sees the full picture.

## Keep an Offline Appointment Card

For every visit, keep a plain-text card with:

| Field | Example of the needed detail |
|---|---|
| Hospital | Official Chinese name, not an informal translation |
| Site | District, campus and street address |
| Clinic | Department plus ordinary, VIP or international service |
| Visit | Date, time window and registration status |
| Patient | Passport name and hospital number |
| Contact | Hospital telephone and named coordinator, if any |
| Preparation | Fasting, medication or specimen instructions confirmed by the clinical team |

One hospital name can cover campuses on opposite sides of the city. Search the street address, not just the institution name. If tests are booked for the same day, leave time for security checks, registration and moving between buildings.

## Plan What Happens After the Doctor Says “Get a Test”

In a public hospital, the doctor's order lands you roughly halfway. You may still have to pay, find the testing area, book a slot, come back for the result and get a second clinical review. Shanghai's municipal step-by-step guide treats registration, consultation, payment, tests and pharmacy as distinct stages,[2] and that matches what happens on the ground.

Before leaving the consultation room, ask:

- Does the test happen today, or does it need its own appointment?
- Do I have to pay before the booking counts?
- Where will the result appear, and under which patient number?
- Does the doctor need to review it in person?
- If the result is urgent, who acts on it?

Your doctor may speak excellent English; the payment counter and the testing desk may not. Keep the test name, the preparation and the location written in Chinese on your phone, or bring a bilingual escort.

## Price the Pathway, Not Just the Consultation

The city's comparison of public and private hospitals points to different service and cost patterns, and it advises booking scarce specialist slots in advance.[3] For an international patient, the registration fee is a small slice of the real total.

Ask for estimates that cover the whole pathway:

- ordinary, VIP or international consultation;
- repeat imaging, laboratory or pathology review;
- a professional medical interpreter;
- hospital deposit and room type;
- physician, anaesthesia, device and medicine charges;
- accommodation for a companion and any extended stay;
- translation and record export after discharge.

On insurance, have the provider submit the intended diagnosis, date, campus and service category. Then ask the insurer whether direct billing covers only approved items and whether you still owe deposits or exclusions. Save both answers. A hospital that “works with your insurer” has not said your claim is authorised — those are two different statements.

## Treat International Service Labels as a Starting Point

Shanghai published a local specification for international medical services in 2024, covering areas such as staffing, quality management and payment models.[7] Useful context. It still won't tell you which clinician is available next month or whether the test you need is included.

So ask operational questions you can actually verify:

- Who is clinically responsible for the case?
- Is the interpreter trained for consent and medication discussions?
- Which records will be provided in English?
- Is there a 24-hour clinical contact after a procedure?
- If the patient transfers, who sends the handover and the images?

## Know What to Do in an Emergency

For a medical emergency in Shanghai, dial 120. The city's emergency guide says the 120 line operates in English; give the exact location, the patient's condition and a callback number, then send someone to guide the ambulance in and clear access.[5]

The same guide notes you may be asked which hospital you want. When minutes matter, follow the dispatcher's guidance and go to an appropriate nearby emergency facility, even if your planned elective provider sits across town. Take the passport, medication list, major allergy information and insurance details — as long as gathering them doesn't delay departure.

Shanghai 12345 is a 24-hour government-service line offered in Chinese and multiple foreign languages.[6] It handles non-emergency city questions well; nobody there will triage you, and a call to it should never come ahead of a 120 call.

## Build the Exit Packet Before Leaving Shanghai

The worst place to discover your imaging only lives inside an app is the airport. Before you leave, ask for:

- the consultation and discharge summary;
- the final medication list, with start, stop and duration instructions;
- laboratory results and the pathology report;
- DICOM imaging plus the radiology report;
- the operative or procedure note when applicable;
- restrictions, warning signs and emergency instructions;
- the follow-up interval, plus a contact who can take questions from your clinician at home;
- an itemised invoice and proof of payment.

Check that every page belongs to the patient and that the dates, laterality and specimen identifiers are right. For continuing care, translate the final record — a preliminary version will only confuse the next doctor.

## A Two-Day Practical Plan

**The day before:** reopen the appointment and confirm the campus, re-check fasting instructions, make sure your payment method works, charge the phone and brief the interpreter on the purpose of the visit.

**The day of care:** bring the passport and the offline appointment card; write down the patient number; photograph no clinical screen without permission; ask how results get back to the doctor; collect receipts before changing buildings.

**After the visit:** compare the new medicine list against what you were taking before, download every result while your access still works, book any review, and send a structured update to your clinician at home.

**Medical disclaimer:** This guide explains common navigation and planning issues in Shanghai. It does not select a provider, establish a diagnosis or replace an individual medical assessment. Service availability and digital access can change. For a medical emergency in Shanghai, call 120.

## FAQ

### Should an international patient use a public or private hospital in Shanghai?

There's no default answer. Weigh the specialty you need, language support, inpatient and emergency backup, timing, cost and your insurer's network. A public hospital's international service is also a genuine third pathway.[1][3]

### Can a foreign passport be used in Suishenban?

Current Shanghai guidance says foreign-passport users can register for specific services, with manual identity review in some cases.[4] Test the account before appointment day and keep the hospital's direct booking route as a fallback.

### Why does the hospital ask me to make a new medical card?

Hospitals commonly open their own patient record for first visits.[2] If you've been before, ask them to check for an earlier number first — duplicate profiles can split your results and follow-up.

### Does direct billing mean I will pay nothing?

No. Direct billing can still leave a deductible, a co-payment, a deposit, an excluded service or an amount above the policy limit. Your insurer must authorise the specific provider and the specific episode.

### Is 12345 the number for an ambulance in Shanghai?

No. Call 120 for a medical emergency. Shanghai 12345 handles non-emergency government-service enquiries and is available in multiple languages.[5][6]
