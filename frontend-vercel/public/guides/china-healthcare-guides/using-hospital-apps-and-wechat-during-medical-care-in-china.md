## Key Takeaways

- Use the exact name, passport number and document type the hospital already has on file. One extra character or a missing space can split your records in two.
- Booking, queue updates, payment, reports and follow-up may all be available online — which ones actually work depends on the hospital, and sometimes on the department.[1][2]
- Keep the appointment confirmation, patient number, cancellation rule and street address saved somewhere offline. The phone is there to help; don't let it become your only way through the door.
- Medical and payment data need extra care. Give a companion access only when the task requires it, and keep reports and QR codes out of ordinary group chats.[3]

## Content

Walk into many Chinese hospitals today and the phone is part of the consultation itself. You book in a mini-program, check in at a kiosk, follow the queue on the hospital app, pay electronically, and get a test-result alert before you go back to the doctor. National policy has pushed hospitals to offer online appointment, payment, navigation, report-query and similar services.[1]

The convenience is real; the uniformity is not. One hospital runs its own app, the next works through a WeChat mini-program, a third sits on a city appointment platform. English menus, passport support and overseas phone-number support vary just as much. So skip the idea of a generic "standard China hospital app" and learn the actual pathway of the one hospital you're going to.

## First, Verify the Digital Channel

A polished icon proves nothing. Get to the account through the hospital's own website, a call to its switchboard or international department, or a QR code displayed at an official hospital counter. Then confirm the Chinese hospital name, the campus and the service telephone.

A hospital group with several campuses may run one account while keeping separate appointment inventories for each. A private clinic with a similar English name may have no connection at all. Before you type in passport or health details, read the privacy notice and make sure the named operator is the hospital itself or a clearly identified authorised service provider.

Store the hospital's regular phone number and street address somewhere outside the app. When the platform goes down, the first thing staff will ask is which channel you booked through and what your appointment number is.

## Create One Patient Identity—and Keep It Consistent

Real-name registration is now standard across China's appointment systems, and regulated internet diagnosis is expressly conducted under a real-name system.[2][4] For foreign patients, the sticking point is rarely the identification itself — it's that the name and document fields don't match from one system to the next.

Register with the same passport you'll show at the hospital. If the form offers "passport" as a document type, choose it; don't squeeze a passport number into a mainland ID-card field unless hospital staff tell you to. Copy the romanised name exactly as the hospital record has it, including the order. Then pick one treatment of your middle name — full, initial or omitted — and use it everywhere.

Once registered, write down:

- Your hospital patient or medical-record number
- The exact name shown in the system
- The passport number and document type you registered with
- The phone number that receives verification codes
- The linked WeChat or app account
- The campus and department

If an old record already exists under a different passport or spelling, ask the registration or records desk whether the two profiles can be linked. Creating yet another profile tends to scatter your tests, prescriptions and bills even further.

## Set It Up Before the Appointment Day

Get the confirmed channel installed and opened while you still have time to fix verification and payment hiccups. Overseas app stores don't always carry every hospital app, and some mini-program pages come in Chinese only.

Without using up a real booking slot, run through these checks:

1. Does the verification code arrive on your phone?
2. Does the system take a passport and an overseas phone number?
3. Can you see the correct hospital campus?
4. Can hospital staff pull up the patient profile?
5. Which payment methods does it accept?
6. Where do cancellations and refunds live?

If the interface won't take a passport, call the hospital. The fix may be an international-department account, manual registration at the counter or a different appointment platform. Making up a Chinese identity number is not a fix.

## What the Phone May Do During a Visit

What the phone handles depends on the hospital, but it can touch several stages:

### Before arrival

Expect to see departments, clinicians, appointment times, instructions and deposits. Screenshot the confirmation, and write down the appointment number and campus as well. A successful booking doesn't always mean you're registered for the day — some hospitals still want you to check in or collect a visit number.

### At the hospital

Here the app may handle check-in, show an electronic visit code, display the queue, guide you through the building or take payment. Arrive when the hospital tells you to. The time on screen is usually a window, so don't count on being called at that exact minute.

### After the consultation

Afterwards the platform may deliver prescriptions, laboratory or imaging reports, follow-up booking, invoices or medication-delivery options. The National Health Commission has encouraged hospitals to tie these online services into the clinical pathway.[1]

Download anything important; don't assume the page will stay up forever. What you see in a portal isn't always the same as an officially stamped medical-record copy or the original DICOM imaging file.

## Booking: Read Four Lines Before Paying

Before you pay, check the clinician, department, campus and appointment date as a set. Then look at the cancellation deadline, the refund route, the late-arrival rule and what the fee actually covers — registration only, or a bigger service package.

Real-name booking exists partly to improve continuity of care and to choke off ticket reselling.[2] Never buy a transferred appointment from an unofficial seller: the identity may fail to match at check-in, and the seller walks away with your personal information.

Unsure which department fits? Ask the hospital's official triage or service desk. A symptom chatbot can help you frame the question; it cannot safely rule out an emergency.

## Payment: Prepare a Backup

Official guidance for overseas visitors says WeChat Pay and Alipay can take supported international cards, though card approval, identity checks, fees and transaction limits all vary.[5] On top of that, each hospital sets its own rules by channel and by type of charge.

Before treatment starts, confirm which of these the hospital takes:

- WeChat Pay or Alipay linked to your card
- An international bank card swiped at a physical terminal
- A Chinese domestic bank card
- Cash in renminbi
- Payment by an authorised companion
- A deposit or an insurer's guarantee

Carry at least two methods that actually work. A card that handled dinner can still be declined on a large hospital deposit because of issuer controls or platform limits. And don't scan money to a clinician's or coordinator's personal QR code unless the hospital has formally confirmed that arrangement and gives you an institutional receipt.

## Results and Messages: Know What Counts as Clinical Advice

A laboratory alert tells you the result is available. It doesn't tell you a clinician has read it. A red flag in the app often just means a value sits outside the laboratory reference range — that alone proves neither an emergency nor a diagnosis.

Ask who will review the result, whether you need to come back to the same-day clinic, and which phone number handles urgent findings. If symptoms are getting worse, a general WeChat service account is the wrong place to wait for help.

In a regulated internet follow-up, the platform should name the medical institution and the clinician, explain the relevant rules and risks, and obtain consent. It should also send you back to in-person care when the case is a first visit, when the condition has materially changed, or when it's otherwise unsuitable for online management.[4]

## Let a Companion Help Without Losing Control

A companion can translate menus, watch the queue and help with payment. Before you start, settle whether they also get to see diagnoses, invoices and prescriptions.

Where possible, the patient's profile stays on the patient's own device, with help run through the platform's authorised family-member function. Don't hand over the main password, the payment PIN or one-time verification codes. If the companion has to use their own phone, note whose account holds the appointment, and ask the hospital how to transfer or unlink that access once the visit is over.

## Privacy: A QR Code Can Carry More Than It Shows

Under China's Personal Information Protection Law, medical-health and financial-account information counts as sensitive personal information, and processing must have a clear purpose and stay within the minimum necessary scope.[3]

In practice:

- Stick to the hospital's verified channel; ignore codes forwarded by unknown accounts
- Check the permissions being requested — location, contacts and photo-library access aren't automatically needed for every function
- Crop or cover patient codes before a screenshot goes to a general chat
- Keep report pages, prescriptions and payment QR codes off social media
- Lock the device and hide medical-message previews on the lock screen
- Cut off a facilitator's or former companion's access when the episode ends
- Ask how to correct a wrong profile or delete the account where that's an option

When clinical records need to move, use the hospital's designated upload function or another secure route you've both agreed on. Chat is convenient, but convenient doesn't mean authorised for identifiable medical files.

## Build an Offline Fallback Card

Keep one image or paper note with:

- Patient name and passport number
- The hospital, campus and department written in Chinese
- The appointment date, time and confirmation number
- The hospital patient number
- The official telephone number
- A coordinator or interpreter contact
- Important allergies and current medicines
- A local emergency contact

Leave the full medical history off a card that could get lost. This card has one job: getting you through reception when the battery dies, the network fails or the app logs you out.

## When Not to Use the App

Severe chest pain, major breathing difficulty, signs of stroke, uncontrolled bleeding, loss of consciousness — emergencies like these need immediate assessment. Waiting for a mini-program reply or the next online slot is not an option.

The same goes for administrative dead ends. If the system won't recognise the passport or has linked the wrong patient, stop repeating payments and have the hospital desk fix the identity before any more clinical data goes in.

**Medical disclaimer:** Digital services smooth out access and administration; they do not replace emergency care, clinical examination or direct instructions from the treating hospital. Functions and rules change, so verify the current process with the named hospital before you rely on it.

## FAQ

### Why can’t the app find my appointment?

Usually it comes down to a different campus, passport type, name order, phone number or patient profile. Line the confirmation up against the identity shown in the hospital system before you create another account.

### Can I use an overseas phone number and bank card?

Some services do, others don't. Chinese official guidance covers linking supported international cards to WeChat Pay and Alipay, but hospital channels and card issuers can add their own conditions.[5]

### Is a test result in the app an official medical record?

Treat it as useful clinical information, but expect that a screen view may not satisfy a receiving doctor, insurer or authority. Ask for the downloadable or stamped version of the report, and get the original imaging files when needed.
