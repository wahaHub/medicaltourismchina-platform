## Key Takeaways

- A hospital-stamped Chinese record, the hospital’s own English summary and an outside translation are three different documents. Before you promise anyone an “English medical record,” find out which of the three the hospital can actually give you.
- Under Chinese rules, patients and authorised representatives can ask for copies of specified medical records once their identity and authority have been checked.[1]
- Even if the hospital offers to write an English summary, still take the full Chinese source set — stamp, page order and original files untouched.
- If the records are going to another doctor, collect the imaging files themselves alongside the reports, plus pathology material or reports, operative and anaesthesia notes, medication details and the discharge summary.

## Content

“Can I get my records in English?” sounds like a single request. Behind the records counter it can mean three different things: a copy of the official Chinese chart, a short English clinical summary written by the treating team, or a translation made after the record comes out.

That difference matters. National rules give patients a way to copy parts of the medical record; they do not promise that every hospital will hand over a complete English version.[1][2] Some international departments write bilingual summaries. Some issue Chinese copies only. A few will point you to an outside translation service. The medical-records office or international patient service can tell you how your hospital handles it.

## Start With the Document’s Next Use

Start by telling the hospital what the document is for. An oncologist abroad needs very different material from a travel insurer or an employer.

For continuing care, ask the receiving doctor which source documents and file formats they actually need. For an insurance claim, get the insurer’s own list and check whether it calls for itemised charges, invoices, a diagnosis certificate, signatures, stamps or a certified translation. Visa, school and employment requests often want yet another form.

Note the purpose on your request list. What you should not do is ask a translator to rework a clinical chart into a letter that argues your case — the translation follows the source, and any summary or opinion belongs to a clinician writing separately.

## Three Documents That Should Not Be Confused

### 1. Hospital-stamped Chinese record copy

This is the source material that comes out through the hospital’s records process. The National Health Commission’s medical-record management rules let patients or authorised agents apply to copy specified inpatient and outpatient materials; the hospital verifies the applicant, checks the copied pages against the record and stamps them.[1]

### 2. Hospital-issued English summary

This might be a discharge summary, a medical certificate or a case synopsis written in English. What is available, what it covers, what it costs and how long it takes all vary by hospital. It works well as an orientation for the receiving side, though it may leave out laboratory results, consent forms or daily notes.

### 3. Translation of the Chinese record

A translator reproduces selected source documents in English. If the receiving organisation asks, the translation can be certified or notarised — certification still does not make it the hospital’s original chart. Keep every translation paired with its Chinese source page.

## What to Collect Before Discharge

Leaving this to the evening before your flight is asking for trouble. Ask the ward or records office when each item becomes final and where you pick it up. A useful transfer set may include:

- Outpatient notes, the admission record and the discharge summary
- Final diagnosis and problem list
- Operation and procedure reports
- Anaesthesia record and recovery notes
- Pathology report with any addenda and biomarker results
- Laboratory and microbiology results, with units and reference ranges
- Imaging reports plus the original CT, MRI, PET, ultrasound or radiography files
- Medication reconciliation: generic name, dose, route, frequency, duration and reason
- Implant or device model, serial or lot information when relevant
- Blood transfusion or allergy information
- Follow-up plan, restrictions and warning signs
- Itemised charges, invoices and payment receipts if needed

The official list of copyable material is broad — orders, surgery and anaesthesia records, pathology, laboratory and imaging reports are all on it.[1] Not everything is final on the day you leave, though. Pathology addenda or send-out tests can arrive later, so fix a contact person and a collection method before you go.

## How to Make the Request

Start with the hospital’s medical-records office, the outpatient records window or the international department. Ask for the application form, the identification requirements, the fee, how you collect the copies and when they will be ready.

The patient normally shows identification. An agent may need the patient’s identification, their own identification and evidence of authorisation or a legal relationship — the exact proof depends on the applicant’s role and the hospital’s procedure.[1] Whatever name the hospital registered, reuse it: the same romanised spelling and identification number.

Specify:

- Hospital name and campus
- Patient name, date of birth and patient or admission number
- Inpatient dates or outpatient visit dates
- Department and treating doctor
- The exact documents you need
- Paper copy, electronic copy, imaging disc or secure download
- Whether a hospital stamp or sealed envelope is required
- Who is allowed to collect the material

Keep the receipt and a copy of the submitted request. Under the national rules, the institution can charge for copying.[1]

## Ask About English—Without Assuming It Is Guaranteed

Ask a precise question: “Can the treating department issue an English discharge summary, and would that be an original hospital document or a translation?” Then pin down what it includes, who signs it, whether it carries the hospital stamp and when it will be ready.

If a full English version is not on offer, take the official Chinese copies first and arrange translation separately, telling the receiving clinician which pages were translated. Where pathology, imaging or medication details will shape care decisions, a short English summary is no substitute for the source evidence.

## Electronic Records and Imaging

Under the national electronic-record rules, hospitals should provide copies in electronic or printed form. An electronic copy should open on its own; a printed copy should be stamped.[2] Where the capability exists, imaging or video material can also be provided electronically.[2]

For scans, ask for the DICOM files — screenshots or phone photos of a monitor will not do. Open the disc or download before you travel. Check the files against the patient, the study date and the body part, and make sure the viewer runs or the raw DICOM folder opens.

A patient-portal link that expires before the receiving appointment is a classic failure point. Within local privacy and hospital rules, save an encrypted working copy and a separate backup.

## Quality-Control the Translation

Give the translator clean source copies plus a glossary of the established English spellings for the patient, hospitals, drugs and prior procedures. Anything illegible should be marked illegible, never guessed.

Go through the high-risk fields line by line:

- Diagnosis, stage, grade and laterality
- Positive versus negative findings
- Drug name, strength, dose, route and schedule
- Decimal points, laboratory units and reference ranges
- Procedure dates and chronology
- Pathology margins, biomarkers and genetic variants
- Negation and uncertainty, such as “cannot exclude”

The translation should name the translator or service, the date, the source language and the pages covered. Page numbering should let anyone match every English page back to the Chinese original. If the source chart itself contains a factual error, ask the hospital about its correction process — changing only the translation just hides the problem.

## Protect Privacy During Delivery

Medical records hold identity and health information together, and the National Health Commission has reiterated that institutions and staff must protect patient privacy and follow the record-copying procedure.[3] Where you can, use a hospital-approved portal, encrypted transfer or tracked physical delivery.

Before files go to a translator, insurer or facilitator, confirm what they need, who can see it, how long they keep it and how you request deletion. Take out irrelevant identification only if the receiving organisation permits redaction. And if a source document is used for clinical care or a formal claim, never alter it without clearly marking the change.

## A Two-Envelope Handover

Picture the final package as two envelopes.

In the **source envelope**: the stamped Chinese record, native laboratory exports, DICOM imaging and original invoices. In the **English envelope**: the hospital’s English summary, clearly labelled translations and a one-page index.

The index lists each document with its date, its language and whether it is an original, hospital-issued English or a translation. It is a small step, and it stops a receiving clinician from reading a translator’s wording as if it were the hospital’s own entry.

## FAQ

### Must a Chinese hospital provide my complete record in English?

National rules give you access to copies of specified medical records; they do not guarantee a complete English edition.[1][2] Ask whether the hospital offers an English clinical summary. If it does not, get the stamped Chinese source copies and arrange a clearly labelled translation.

### Can a family member collect the records for me?

Often yes — provided the hospital accepts the arrangement and your representative brings the required identity and authorisation documents. Get the exact checklist before anyone makes the trip.[1]

### Is an English discharge summary enough for a second opinion?

Usually not on its own. The reviewing specialist may also want pathology, laboratory results, imaging reports, native DICOM files, operation notes and treatment details. Ask the reviewer before you order anything.

### What if a pathology addendum arrives after I leave China?

Before you are discharged, designate an authorised contact, confirm the release channel and ask how the hospital will verify identity remotely. While additional testing is still pending, a preliminary pathology report is not the final word.
