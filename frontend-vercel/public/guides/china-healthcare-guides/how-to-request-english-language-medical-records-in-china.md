## Key Takeaways

- A Chinese source record, a hospital-issued English summary and a third-party translation are different documents. Ask which one is available before promising an “English medical record.”
- Chinese rules allow patients and authorised representatives to request copies of specified medical records after identity and authority are verified.[1]
- Request the complete Chinese source set even if the hospital can prepare an English summary. Keep the hospital stamp, page order and original files intact.
- For transfer of care, collect imaging files as well as reports, pathology material or reports, operative and anaesthesia notes, medication details and a discharge summary.
- Translation should reproduce names, numbers, dates, units and uncertainty faithfully. It must not silently “correct” or reinterpret the source record.

## Content

“Can I get my records in English?” sounds like one request. At a hospital records counter it may describe three quite different things: a copy of the official Chinese chart, a brief English clinical summary written by the treating team, or a translation produced after the record is released.

The distinction matters. National rules describe a patient’s right to copy parts of the medical record, but they do not establish a general promise that every hospital must issue a complete English version.[1][2] Some international departments prepare bilingual summaries; some provide only Chinese copies; some may suggest an external translation service. Confirm the local process with the medical-records office or international patient service.

## Start With the Document’s Next Use

Tell the hospital why the document is needed. A receiving oncologist needs different material from a travel insurer or an employer.

For continuing clinical care, ask the receiving doctor which source documents and file formats are essential. For an insurance claim, request the insurer’s list and ask whether it requires itemised charges, invoices, a diagnosis certificate, signatures, stamps or a certified translation. Visa, school and employment requests may use yet another form.

Write the purpose on your request list, but do not ask the translator to turn a clinical chart into an advocacy letter. A translation should reflect the source; a separate clinician can write a summary or opinion.

## Three Documents That Should Not Be Confused

### 1. Hospital-stamped Chinese record copy

This is the source material released through the hospital’s records process. Under the National Health Commission’s medical-record management rules, patients or authorised agents may apply to copy specified inpatient and outpatient materials. Hospitals verify the applicant, check the copied pages against the record and stamp the copies.[1]

### 2. Hospital-issued English summary

This may be a discharge summary, medical certificate or case synopsis written in English. Availability, scope, fee and turnaround vary. An English summary is useful for orientation but may not contain every laboratory result, consent form or daily note.

### 3. Translation of the Chinese record

A translator reproduces selected source documents in English. The translation may be certified or notarised if the receiving organisation asks for that, but certification does not transform it into the hospital’s original chart. Keep each translation paired with its Chinese source page.

## What to Collect Before Discharge

Do not wait until the evening before a flight. Ask the ward or records office when each item becomes final and where it is released. A useful transfer set may include:

- Outpatient notes, admission record and discharge summary
- Final diagnosis and problem list
- Operation and procedure reports
- Anaesthesia record and recovery notes
- Pathology report, including addenda and biomarker results
- Laboratory and microbiology results with units and reference ranges
- Imaging reports plus original CT, MRI, PET, ultrasound or radiography files
- Medication reconciliation: generic name, dose, route, frequency, duration and reason
- Implant or device model, serial or lot information when relevant
- Blood transfusion or allergy information
- Follow-up plan, restrictions and warning signs
- Itemised charges, invoices and payment receipts if needed

The official list of copyable material is broad and includes records such as orders, surgery and anaesthesia records, pathology, laboratory and imaging reports.[1] Not every item is final on the discharge date. Pathology addenda or send-out tests may arrive later, so identify a contact and collection method before leaving.

## How to Make the Request

Contact the hospital’s medical-records office, outpatient records window or international department. Ask for its application form, identification requirements, fee, collection method and expected completion date.

The patient will normally need identification. An agent may need the patient’s identification, the agent’s identification and evidence of authorisation or legal relationship; the exact proof depends on the applicant’s role and the hospital’s procedure.[1] Use the same romanised name and identification number that the hospital used to register the patient.

Specify:

- Hospital name and campus
- Patient name, date of birth and patient or admission number
- Inpatient dates or outpatient visit dates
- Department and treating doctor
- Exact documents requested
- Paper copy, electronic copy, imaging disc or secure download
- Whether a hospital stamp or sealed envelope is required
- Who may collect the material

Keep the receipt and a copy of the submitted request. Medical institutions may charge copying costs under the national rules.[1]

## Ask About English—Without Assuming It Is Guaranteed

Use a precise question: “Can the treating department issue an English discharge summary, and is it an original hospital document or a translation?” Then ask what it includes, who signs it, whether it carries the hospital stamp and when it will be ready.

If a full English version is unavailable, request the official Chinese copies first. Arrange translation separately and tell the receiving clinician which pages were translated. A short English summary should not replace source pathology, imaging or medication evidence when those details affect care.

## Electronic Records and Imaging

National electronic-record rules say hospitals should provide copies in electronic or printed form; an electronic copy should be independently readable, while a printed copy should be stamped.[2] Where capability exists, imaging or video material may also be provided electronically.[2]

For scans, ask for DICOM files rather than screenshots or photographs of a monitor. Open the disc or download before travelling. Confirm that the files belong to the correct patient, study date and body part, and that the viewer works or the raw DICOM folder is accessible.

Do not rely on a patient-portal link that expires before the receiving appointment. Save an encrypted working copy and a separate backup, subject to local privacy and hospital rules.

## Quality-Control the Translation

Give the translator clean source copies and a glossary of established English spellings for the patient, hospitals, drugs and prior procedures. Ask that illegible text be marked as illegible rather than guessed.

Review high-risk fields line by line:

- Diagnosis, stage, grade and laterality
- Positive versus negative findings
- Drug name, strength, dose, route and schedule
- Decimal points, laboratory units and reference ranges
- Procedure dates and chronology
- Pathology margins, biomarkers and genetic variants
- Negation and uncertainty, such as “cannot exclude”

The translation should identify the translator or service, date, source language and translated pages. Page numbers should make it possible to match every English page to the Chinese original. If a factual error exists in the source chart, ask the hospital about its correction process; do not hide it by changing only the translation.

## Protect Privacy During Delivery

Medical records contain identity and health information. The National Health Commission has reiterated that institutions and staff must protect patient privacy and follow the record-copying procedure.[3] Use a hospital-approved portal, encrypted transfer or tracked physical delivery where available.

Before sending files to a translator, insurer or facilitator, confirm what they need, who can access it, how long it will be retained and how deletion can be requested. Remove irrelevant identification only when the receiving organisation permits redaction; never alter a source document used for clinical care or a formal claim without clearly marking the change.

## A Two-Envelope Handover

Think of the final package as two envelopes.

The **source envelope** holds the stamped Chinese record, native laboratory exports, DICOM imaging and original invoices. The **English envelope** holds the hospital’s English summary, clearly labelled translations and a one-page index.

On the index, list each document, date, language and whether it is original, hospital-issued English or translated. This small step prevents a receiving clinician from treating a translator’s wording as if it were the hospital’s original entry.

**Medical disclaimer:** This guide provides general administrative and communication information, not legal or medical advice. Record-access procedures, available languages and document requirements vary by institution and intended use. Confirm the current rules with the hospital and the receiving clinician, insurer or authority.

## Related Hospitals

Ask the specific hospital’s medical-records office and international department about English summaries, record-copying hours, identity documents, fees and electronic formats. Do not infer these services from a hospital ranking or an English-language website.

For surgery, cancer care, transplantation and implanted devices, ask the receiving clinician for a document checklist early; pathology, imaging and device records may take longer to assemble than a general discharge summary.

## FAQ

### Must a Chinese hospital provide my complete record in English?

National rules support access to copies of specified medical records, but do not create a general guarantee of a complete English edition.[1][2] Ask whether the hospital offers an English clinical summary; otherwise obtain stamped Chinese source copies and arrange a clearly labelled translation.

### Can a family member collect the records for me?

Often yes, if the hospital accepts the arrangement and the representative provides the required identity and authorisation documents. Ask for the exact checklist before the representative travels to the hospital.[1]

### Is an English discharge summary enough for a second opinion?

Usually not by itself. The reviewing specialist may also need pathology, laboratory results, imaging reports, native DICOM files, operation notes and treatment details. Ask the reviewer first.

### Should I translate every page?

Not necessarily. Ask the receiving clinician or insurer which documents are material. Always retain the complete source set and clearly state which pages were not translated.

### What if a pathology addendum arrives after I leave China?

Before discharge, designate an authorised contact, confirm the release channel and ask how identity will be verified remotely. Do not treat a preliminary pathology report as final when additional testing is pending.
